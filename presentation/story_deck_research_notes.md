# Story Deck: research notes and design rules

What the evidence says about making a 10-minute statistics talk land, distilled into rules that `Story_Deck.html` follows. Searches were run on 2026-11-05. Where I could only see an abstract, a publisher page or a secondary summary, the "Strength" column says so. Nothing here is invented. Sources I knew already and did not re-check online in this session are marked *(not re-verified)*.

## Strength scale

- **Strong:** controlled experiments or meta-analyses in peer-reviewed venues.
- **Moderate:** a few experiments, small samples, or one research group.
- **Practitioner:** books by experienced designers or communicators. Useful craft, not experimental evidence.

## The 10 rules

| # | Rule | Evidence (strength) | How the deck applies it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Every headline is a full-sentence claim; the body is visual evidence for it** (assertion-evidence). | Garner & Alley: in a two-group experiment (55 vs 56 students, same recorded talk), assertion-evidence slides gave better comprehension of complex concepts, and an engineering sample (n = 110) showed fewer misconceptions and lower perceived cognitive load. *Moderate:* small samples, mostly one research group, and topic-subtopic slides were not worse on simple recall. | All 14 main slides have a sentence headline ("Fair comparisons shrink the study effect from +0.97 to +0.05") and one dominant chart. No bullet-list slides in the main flow. |
| 2 | **Cut everything that doesn't serve the claim** (coherence) and **don't read text that is on screen** (redundancy). | Mayer (2017) review of multimedia principles: coherence d = 0.70, redundancy d = 0.87, signalling d = 0.46, segmenting d = 0.70. *Strong for the principles, but the effect sizes come from one review, and effects are larger for novices.* | Test statistics sit in a small "method" strip, not the headline. The speaker script says the claim; the slide shows the evidence. Full tables go to backup slides. |
| 3 | **Signal the one thing to look at:** one accent colour, used only for the key insight. | Mayer signalling principle (above). Tufte's data-ink idea (*Practitioner*) has mixed empirical support: Inbar, Tractinsky & Meyer (2007, n = 87) found people *preferred* the less minimal bar charts. So the aim is "nothing distracting", not "as bare as possible". | Neutral grey for context data. Amber appears only for the insight on each slide (the 2.38 gap, the coefficient that is left, the 15 zero-grade cases, the corrected estimate). Charts keep light gridlines and direct labels rather than being stripped bare. |
| 4 | **Show a few chunks at a time; build complex charts step by step** (segmenting, working memory). | Sweller (1988) cognitive load theory; Cowan (2001): working memory holds about 4 chunks, not Miller's 7 ± 2. Mayer segmenting d = 0.70. *Strong.* | The ladder, the paradox scatter and the A/B reveal each advance on a click, one idea per click. No slide shows more than about four things to compare. |
| 5 | **Open with a gap in knowledge the audience wants closed** (curiosity gap). | Loewenstein (1994), *Psychological Bulletin*: curiosity comes from noticing a gap in what you know, and it needs some prior knowledge. *Strong theory paper; I read the abstract and secondary summaries, not the full text.* | Slide 1 asks for a number before showing any data ("how many points apart?"). Act 3 opens with a result that should feel wrong ("missing class barely matters?") before it is explained. |
| 6 | **Make the audience commit to a prediction before the reveal.** | Brod (2021), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*: a review arguing prediction is a learning strategy whose benefit comes largely from surprise. Brod, Hasselhorn & Bunge (2018) found the benefit in an experiment. St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn (2023) meta-analysis of prequestions: g = 0.54 for the questioned material, about 0 for other material. *Moderate to strong; the benefit is item-specific.* | Two votes: the opening bet (the class's guess is typed in and shown again on slide 3 next to the real 2.38) and Student A vs Student B (hands up, tallied on screen, then revealed). Both target the exact facts we want remembered. |
| 7 | **Build to one peak, and end deliberately** (peak-end). | Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier (1993), *Psychological Science*: people judged an episode by its worst moment and its end, largely ignoring duration (cold-water study). *Strong for remembered experience; applying it to talks is an analogy, not a tested result.* | The peak is the Perfect Attendance Paradox reveal (slide 8, 15 of 15 light up). The end is a three-line verdict plus one sentence to take home: "Before you believe a regression, check its zeros." Limitations come just before the verdict (slide 13), so the last thing heard is the take-home line, not an apology. |
| 8 | **Use a story structure, but keep it honest.** | Dahlstrom (2014), *PNAS*: narratives increase comprehension and engagement for non-experts, but audiences find it hard to spot errors inside a story. Duarte, *Resonate* (2010): alternate "what is" and "what could be". Heath & Heath, *Made to Stick* (2007): simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, stories. *Moderate (Dahlstrom review) / Practitioner (books).* | A detective case in three acts with a twist. Because stories hide errors (Dahlstrom), every act ends with a "what this does *not* show" line, and the dropout reading is labelled as our interpretation every time it appears. |
| 9 | **Concrete beats abstract: use people and pictures, not just coefficients.** | Picture superiority / dual coding (Paivio & Csapo 1973; Paivio 1971): pictures are remembered better than words. *Strong for recognition; the mechanism is debated.* Borkin et al. (2013): memorable charts are not necessarily better understood. *Moderate.* | Coefficients are turned into grade points for real contrasts ("one past failure: -1.42 points"). Two named profiles (Student A, Student B) carry the model. The 15 zero-grade students are drawn as 15 individual lines. |
| 10 | **For a technical audience, show the estimate and its uncertainty as a graph, and say why each method was chosen.** | Kastellec & Leoni (2007), *Perspectives on Politics*: graphs of estimates and uncertainties communicate regression results better than tables. Cleveland & McGill (1984), *JASA*: position on a common scale is read more accurately than length, angle or area *(not re-verified)*. Gelman & Nolan, *Teaching Statistics: A Bag of Tricks* (2nd ed. 2017): active participation in statistics teaching. *Moderate / Practitioner.* | Dot-and-whisker and dumbbell charts with 95% CIs instead of tables. Bars are used only from a zero baseline. Every method slide has a "Why we did it this way" beat tied to a course concept (Tukey after ANOVA, HC3, log(abs+1), ANCOVA, with and without zero grades, G1/G2 as bad controls). |

## Two cautions taken from the evidence

- **Redundancy cuts both ways.** Mayer's redundancy effect is about printed text that duplicates narration. Some classmates may be listening in a second language, so each headline stays on screen as a single anchor sentence. What the deck avoids is paragraphs.
- **Embellishment.** Bateman et al. (2010, "Useful Junk?") found that embellished charts were remembered better without being understood worse. The deck uses that only lightly (a glow on the 15 cases, the melting ladder) and never adds decoration that encodes nothing.

**Honest summary.** The strongest evidence supports rules 2, 4 and 6 (multimedia principles, cognitive load, prediction). Rule 1 rests on a small number of studies, mostly from one group. Rules 7 and 8 apply findings from memory and science communication to talks by analogy. The design books are craft advice, not evidence.
